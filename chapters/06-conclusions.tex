In this final chapter, we conclude the thesis by returning to the questions that motivated the work. We first summarize the contribution of each of the three studies presented in the preceding chapters, and then draw together their results into the combined physical picture developed in this thesis. We end by stating the main conclusion of the thesis and its implications for compact stellar systems, environmental processing, and galaxy evolution in dense cluster environments.

\section{Motivation and Guiding Questions}

Throughout this thesis, we have used \glspl{css} as long-lived tracers of galaxy assembly and environmental processing. \Glspl{gc} and \glspl{ucd} can survive for many billions of years, and as a result they preserve information about the dynamical histories of their host galaxies and the dense environments in which those galaxies evolve. The primary aim of this work was to investigate how the environment of the Coma Cluster core has influenced the evolution of its member galaxies, using \glspl{css} as discrete tracers of stripping, redistribution, and intracluster assembly.

The key questions guiding this thesis were therefore complementary. What signatures of environmental stripping are imprinted on the \glspl{gcs} of galaxies in the Coma Cluster core? How do asymmetry, color, and local density help distinguish between different modes of environmental processing? And what role does the cluster environment play in shaping the broader \gls{css} population, including \glspl{gc}, \glspl{ucd}, and the intracluster component?

\section{Building the Environmental Picture}

In our first paper (\cref{ch:chapter1}ch:paper-example), we focused on the \gls{ucd} population in the Coma Cluster core. We examined the bright end of the \gls{gclf} and then explored the spatial distribution of \glspl{ucd} as a function of luminosity and color. This analysis identified an excess at the bright end of the \gls{css} population relative an extrapolation of the \gls{gclf}, suggesting that \glspl{ucd} are likely to form through multiple pathways. In particular, this result supports the interpretation that the \gls{ucd} population may be augmented by stripped nuclei produced through environmental processing. We also found that, similar to \glspl{gc}, redder, metal-rich \glspl{ucd} are more centrally concentrated around galaxies, while bluer, metal-poor \glspl{ucd} are more spatially extended. Together, these results provided evidence that environmental processing has influenced the \gls{css} population in the Coma Cluster core, but they did not identify the specific mechanisms responsible. This first study therefore established that the \gls{css} population carries signatures of both environmental processing and population complexity, while also showing that a galaxy-by-galaxy analysis was needed to identify where those signatures were most strongly expressed. In addition, the study revealed hints of galaxies with deficient \gls{gc} populations, motivating the more detailed analysis carried out in our second paper.

In \cref{ch:paper2} with our second paper, we moved from the cluster-wide \gls{css} population to the \glspl{gcs} of individual galaxies. Using \gls{gc} specific-frequency expectations based on galaxy absolute $V$-band luminosity, we predicted the \gls{gc} populations of major galaxies in the Coma Cluster core and compared these expectations with the observed \gls{gc} density field using two-dimensional Voronoi-based density maps. This analysis identified several galaxies with ach:chapter4nomalously low \gls{gc} populations relative to their expected counts. We then examined the azimuthal structure of these deficient \glspl{gcs}, finding a wide range of behavior, from strongly anisotropic systems to nearly symmetric ones. These results provided further evidence that environmental processing affects individual galaxy \glspl{gcs}, potentially through stripping or redistribution of their outer \gls{gc} populations. However, the diversity of asymmetry signatures also highlighted an important degeneracy: deficient systems may reflect recent stripping, older phase-mixed processing, symmetric truncation, or intrinsically low \gls{gc} formation efficiency. This motivated our third study, in which we introduced \gls{gc} color as an additional diagnostic of environmental processing. This study therefore established that \gls{gc} loss is not a uniform process, but varies from galaxy to galaxy in a way that likely reflects differences in local environment, orbital history, and timescale.

In our third study (\cref{ch:chapter4}), we introduced the blue-\gls{gc} fraction as a diagnostic of the metallicity mix of galaxy \glspl{gcs}. We compared this diagnostic with environmental measures on several physical scales, including cluster-centric radius, the influence of the dual \glspl{bcg}, and the local \gls{gc} density field. We also examined how blue- and red-\gls{gc} subpopulation asymmetries relate to the inferred metallicity mix. The strongest environmental trends were associated with local \gls{gc} density and, to a lesser extent, \gls{bcg}-related metrics, while trends with cluster-centric radius were comparatively weak. These results indicate that the Coma Cluster core is not well described as a smooth, radially isotropic environment. Instead, the metallicity mix of galaxy \glspl{gcs}, especially through variations in the blue-\gls{gc} fraction, appears most sensitive to local environmental structure. This final study therefore showed that the blue, metal-poor \gls{gc} population provides the clearest tracer of environmental processing in the Coma Cluster core. Together with the \gls{gc} deficits and asymmetries identified in \cref{ch:paper-example}, this result motivated the physical picture developed in \cref{ch:discussion} of the Coma Cluster core: \glspl{css} preserve signatures of stripping, redistribution, and population-dependent processing across multiple physical scales.

\section{A Combined Physical Picture}

Taken together, the three studies presented in this thesis support a coherent picture of environmental processing in the Coma Cluster core. This picture is not a single evolutionary pathway followed by every galaxy, but rather a set of linked processes that act with different strengths, geometries, and timescales. The common thread is that the dense cluster environment acts most efficiently on the extended and weakly bound components of galaxies. Outer blue \glspl{gc}, diffuse stellar halos, and low-mass or nucleated satellites are therefore expected to be affected before the more tightly bound central stellar bodies, red \gls{gc} populations, and compact nuclei.

In this framework, the results of the thesis trace both removal and redistribution. The bright end of the \gls{css} population points to a composite \gls{ucd} population, including a possible contribution from stripped nuclei. The deficient \glspl{gcs} identified around individual galaxies show that some systems have fewer \glspl{gc} than expected for their luminosities, while their diverse azimuthal structures suggest that this loss is not uniform in geometry or timescale. Strongly asymmetric systems may preserve recent or directional signatures, whereas smoother deficits may reflect older phase-mixed processing, approximately symmetric truncation, intrinsically low \gls{gc} formation efficiency, or pre-processing before the galaxy entered the present core. The color-based analysis then identifies the blue, metal-poor \gls{gc} population as the component most sensitive to local environmental structure.

This picture also has implications for the underlying \gls{dm} halos and for the dynamical state of the cluster core. If the extended blue \gls{gc} population has been stripped or truncated, then the still more extended outer \gls{dm} halos of these galaxies are also likely to have been affected by the cluster tidal field. The \gls{css} signatures identified here therefore suggest that some galaxies may retain relatively intact central stellar bodies while already having lost part of their outer halo structure. At the cluster scale, the non-uniform \gls{css} distribution, the importance of local \gls{gc} density and \gls{bcg}-related metrics, and the presence of asymmetric or bridge-like structures all point to a dynamically structured core rather than a fully relaxed, radially isotropic system. Some of this structure may arise from ongoing processing within the Coma Cluster core, while some may reflect earlier pre-processing in infalling groups or substructures.

The same physical picture also links galaxy-scale transformation to cluster-scale assembly. \Glspl{gc} stripped from galaxy halos are not lost from the cluster; they are redistributed into the intracluster environment, where they contribute to the local \gls{gc} density field and the broader \gls{icgc} population. Similarly, the stripping of diffuse stellar envelopes contributes to the \gls{icl}, while dense galactic nuclei may survive as \glspl{ucd}. The \gls{css} population therefore records both sides of environmental processing: the depletion and reshaping of individual galaxy halos, and the buildup of intracluster stellar material.

The combined physical picture can therefore be summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{The environment is structured.} The Coma Cluster core is not well described by cluster-centric radius alone, but by a multi-scale field shaped by the global cluster potential, the dual \glspl{bcg}, local overdensities, and substructure.

    \item \textit{The response is hierarchical.} Environmental processing acts most efficiently on the extended and weakly bound components of galaxies, with blue, metal-poor \glspl{gc} providing the clearest tracer of this response.

    \item \textit{The signatures are complementary.} \Gls{gc} deficits, azimuthal asymmetries, and blue-fraction variations trace different aspects of stripping, redistribution, pre-processing, phase mixing, and intrinsic \gls{gc} formation differences.

    \item \textit{Galaxy evolution and cluster assembly are coupled.} Stripped \glspl{gc}, diffuse stellar material, and surviving compact nuclei link the transformation of individual galaxies to the buildup of the \gls{icgc}, \gls{icl}, and \gls{ucd} populations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Main Conclusion}

The central conclusion of this thesis is that \glspl{css} in the Coma Cluster core preserve measurable signatures of environmental processing, and that these signatures are structured, local, and population-dependent. The strongest evidence does not support a simple radially isotropic, virialized picture in which galaxy evolution is governed solely by distance from the cluster center. Instead, the results point to a multi-scale environment shaped by the global cluster potential, the dual \glspl{bcg}, local \gls{gc} density, and galaxy-scale structure.

Within this environment, the blue, metal-poor \gls{gc} population provides the clearest tracer of environmental influence. Its extended spatial distribution makes it especially sensitive to stripping and redistribution, allowing changes in the \gls{gcs} metallicity mix to reveal processing that may not be obvious from total \gls{gc} counts alone. At the same time, asymmetries and deficits in individual \glspl{gcs} show that environmental processing is heterogeneous, with galaxies observed at different stages of stripping, truncation, pre-processing, or phase mixing.

Thus, the main result of this thesis is not simply that the Coma Cluster environment affects \glspl{css}, but that \glspl{css} reveal how that environment acts: locally rather than only radially, preferentially on extended blue populations, and through redistribution into the intracluster component. In this way, \glspl{css} provide a bridge between the evolution of individual galaxies and the assembly of the cluster-scale stellar environment.

\section{Looking Forward}

The next step is to connect the projected \gls{css} signatures identified here to full dynamical and multi-wavelength histories. Spectroscopic velocities for \glspl{gc} and \glspl{ucd} would separate galaxy-bound, \gls{bcg}-halo, and intracluster populations, while stellar-population measurements would help distinguish massive star clusters from stripped nuclei. Wider-field imaging with facilities such as Euclid and Roman would place the HST/ACS Coma core observations into a broader spatial context, linking compact-object structure to the \gls{icl}, cluster outskirts, and large-scale environment. Finally, simulations that track stars, gas, \gls{dm}, and \gls{gc} subpopulations together will be needed to test whether the observed deficits, asymmetries, blue-fraction trends, and intracluster structures arise naturally from stripping, pre-processing, and phase mixing.

The work presented in this thesis therefore provides a foundation for using \glspl{css} as tracers of environmental processing in dense galaxy clusters. In the Coma Cluster, these systems reveal not only what has been removed from galaxies, but also what has been built in the space between them.
